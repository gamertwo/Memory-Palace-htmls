Derivation

Electric Field on the Axis of a Charged Ring

This derivation is the chapter's cleanest symmetry win. Every tiny charge element on the ring creates a slanted field at the axis point, but opposite elements cancel sideways pieces and leave only the axial component. The integral then collapses because every geometric factor is constant around the ring.

$$E_z=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Qz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$$

Rooms: Full Derivation

Room 1: Choose One Tiny Charge Piece on the Ring

  • Take one small ring element with charge $$dq=\lambda\,ds.$$
  • That tiny piece acts like a point charge for the purpose of writing $$d\vec E.$$
  • The observation point sees it along a slanted line.

Why This Step

The ring is a continuous object, so we begin by shrinking it into one small source piece. This is the standard Chapter 22 bridge from a real shape to Coulomb's law.

Room 2: Write the Geometry Once

  • The distance from any ring element to the axis point is the same:
  • $$r=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}.$$
  • The same geometry gives $$\cos\theta=z/r.$$

Why This Step

The ring is special because every source element has the same distance to the axis point. That uniformity is why the integral becomes much cleaner than a rod integral.

Room 3: Write $$dE$$ and Keep Only the Axial Part

  • $$dE=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{dq}{r^2}.$$
  • The surviving axial component is $$dE_z=dE\cos\theta.$$
  • So $$dE_z=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{z\,dq}{r^3}.$$

Why This Step

Each tiny field is slanted, so we project it onto the axis. This is the exact moment where symmetry tells us which part deserves to be integrated.

Room 4: Use Symmetry to Kill the Sideways Parts

  • Opposite ring elements produce equal transverse components in opposite directions.
  • Those sideways parts cancel around the ring.
  • Therefore the axis component is the only piece that survives the full sum.

Why This Step

Without symmetry, this would be a vector integral in changing directions. Symmetry collapses the whole problem into one scalar integral along the axis.

Room 5: Integrate Around the Ring

  • Because $$r$$ and $$z$$ are constant around the ring, they come outside the integral.
  • So $$E_z=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{z}{r^3}\int dq.$$
  • And $$\int dq=Q.$$

Why This Step

This is the payoff of the ring geometry: the integral is no longer about changing distance or changing angle. It is only about summing all the charge.

Room 6: Write the Final Ring Result

  • Substitute $$r^2=R^2+z^2.$$
  • The final field is $$E_z=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{Qz}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.$$
  • The field points along the symmetry axis.

Why This Step

The last rewrite expresses the answer only in source quantities and observation distance. That is the clean form you can later reuse when building a disk from rings.
