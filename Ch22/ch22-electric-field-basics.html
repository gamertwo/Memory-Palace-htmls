Electric Field Basics

This page locks the core idea of the whole chapter: a source charge changes the space around it, and that change is called the electric field. Once the field is known, any later problem becomes a question of how to add fields, derive a field from a charge distribution, or turn field into force on a particle.

$$\vec E=\frac{\vec F}{q_0},\qquad \vec F=q\vec E,\qquad \vec E_{\text{point}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat r$$

Quick Data

The field is the force-per-unit-charge pattern set up by the sources.

Rooms: Core Idea

Room 1: A Source Charge Changes the Space Around It

  • A charged particle does not need contact to matter physically.
  • Instead it creates an electric field in the surrounding region.
  • The chapter is mostly about finding that field for different source shapes.

Why This Step

If we do not first accept field as a property of space, every later derivation looks like magic. This step tells us what we are trying to calculate on every page.

Room 2: Probe the Field with a Tiny Positive Test Charge

  • Place a very small positive test charge $$q_0$$ at the observation point.
  • If the sources exert force $$\vec F$$ on it, that force reveals the field at that point.
  • The test charge must be small so it does not noticeably alter the original arrangement.

Why This Step

The field itself is invisible. The test charge is the measuring tool that lets us detect direction and strength without redefining the source configuration.

Room 3: Define the Electric Field Cleanly

  • The field is defined by $$\vec E=\vec F/q_0.$$
  • This ratio strips away the chosen probe and leaves a property of the sources alone.
  • That is why the same field can later act on any other charge.

Why This Step

We divide by the test charge because we want the source contribution only. This keeps the idea reusable instead of tied to one particular probe.

Room 4: Turn Field Back into Force on a Real Particle

  • Rearranging gives the working relation $$\vec F=q\vec E.$$
  • A positive charge feels force along $$\vec E,$$ while a negative charge feels force opposite $$\vec E.$$
  • So field direction and force direction are the same only for positive charges.

Why This Step

This is the bridge from pure field derivations to real examples. Once we know the field, applications like acceleration, motion, and torque become possible.

Room 5: Lock the Point-Charge Formula

  • For one point source, $$\vec E=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{q}{r^2}\hat r.$$
  • The field grows with source charge and weakens with the square of distance.
  • This inverse-square pattern is the atomic building block for the rest of Chapter 22.

Why This Step

All continuous-charge derivations are really just the point-charge law applied to one tiny piece at a time. This formula is the seed from which every integral page grows.
