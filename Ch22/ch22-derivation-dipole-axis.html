Electric Field on the Axis of a Dipole

This page is about the field produced by the dipole itself, not a dipole placed in an external field. The whole derivation is a subtraction movie: place the observation point on the axis, write the two point-charge fields, subtract them carefully, then use the far-field idea to reveal the dipole-moment form.

$$E_{\text{axis, far}}\approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2p}{z^3},\qquad p=qd$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Geometry
Charges $$+q$$ and $$-q$$ are separated by distance $$d.$$
Observation Point
Point lies on the axis a distance $$z$$ from the dipole center.
Exact Theme
Two unequal distances create a field difference, not a cancellation.
Far Limit
When $$z\gg d,$$ the field becomes a pure dipole field.
Rooms: Full Derivation

Room 1: Place the Point on the Dipole Axis

  • The observation point is not equally far from the two charges.
  • The distance to $$+q$$ is $$z-d/2,$$ while the distance to $$-q$$ is $$z+d/2.$$
  • That difference is why the two field magnitudes do not cancel.

Why This Step

The whole derivation depends on getting the geometry right first. If the distances are wrong, every later subtraction is wrong even if the algebra is neat.

Room 2: Write the Two Point-Charge Fields

  • The field from $$+q$$ points away from $$+q.$$
  • The field from $$-q$$ points toward $$-q.$$
  • Along the axis these contributions oppose, so the net field is a difference of magnitudes.

Why This Step

A dipole is still just two point charges. We write the simple point-charge fields first because the dipole formula is not something to memorize blindly; it is something we build from the basic law.

Room 3: Form the Exact Axis Expression

  • $$E=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{q}{(z-d/2)^2}-\frac{q}{(z+d/2)^2}\right].$$
  • Both terms are along the axis, so no component split is needed.
  • What remains is pure algebra.

Why This Step

Once the directions are settled, the derivation becomes a one-dimensional subtraction. This is why axis problems are the easiest place to meet dipole fields for the first time.

Room 4: Combine the Fractions

  • Combining the two terms gives
  • $$E=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2qzd}{(z^2-d^2/4)^2}.$$
  • This is the exact axis field before any approximation.

Why This Step

This step exposes the exact structure of the dipole field. We do the full algebra first so the far-field limit later feels like a justified simplification, not a guess.

Room 5: Use the Far-Field Limit

  • When $$z\gg d,$$ the term $$d^2/4$$ is tiny compared with $$z^2.$$
  • So $$\left(z^2-d^2/4\right)^2\approx z^4.$$
  • This gives $$E\approx \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2qd}{z^3}.$$

Why This Step

Far away, the two charges look like one compact dipole rather than two separate particles. The approximation tells us what feature survives at large distance.

Room 6: Replace $$qd$$ by Dipole Moment

  • Define the dipole moment magnitude as $$p=qd.$$
  • Then the far-axis field becomes $$E\approx \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2p}{z^3}.$$
  • The key memory result is that dipole fields fall as $$1/z^3,$$ faster than point-charge fields.

Why This Step

The symbol $$p$$ compresses the source structure into one physical quantity. That makes the final formula cleaner and reveals the signature dipole scaling law immediately.
