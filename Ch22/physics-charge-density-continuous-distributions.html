Charge Density and Continuous Distributions

This page fills the bridge concept needed by the later ring, disk, and rod derivations: how to turn a spread-out charge into tiny pieces, write $$dq$$ correctly, build $$d\vec E,$$ and then integrate the surviving components.

$$\lambda=\frac{dq}{ds},\qquad \sigma=\frac{dq}{dA},\qquad \rho=\frac{dq}{dV}$$
Charge Measures
Charge
Total charge $$q$$ is measured in coulombs.
Linear Density
$$\lambda$$ is charge per unit length for rods, arcs, and rings.
Surface Density
$$\sigma$$ is charge per unit area for disks and sheets.
Volume Density
$$\rho$$ is charge per unit volume for three-dimensional distributions.
Rooms

Room 1: A Spread-Out Object Is Not a Point Charge

  • A rod, ring, disk, or solid body cannot be handled as one point source immediately.
  • You first carve out one tiny element.
  • That element is small enough to behave like a point charge.

Room 2: Choose the Right Density

  • Use $$\lambda$$ for one-dimensional charge distributions.
  • Use $$\sigma$$ for surfaces.
  • Use $$\rho$$ for volumes.

Room 3: Convert the Element into Charge

  • Line element: $$dq=\lambda\,ds.$$
  • Area element: $$dq=\sigma\,dA.$$
  • Volume element: $$dq=\rho\,dV.$$

Room 4: Build the Differential Field

  • Treat that tiny charge element as a point source.
  • Then
  • $$d\vec E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r.$$
  • Only after writing $$d\vec E$$ do you substitute the proper expression for $$dq.$$

Room 5: Use Geometry and Symmetry

  • Geometry supplies distances, angles, and component relations.
  • Symmetry often cancels pairs of sideways components.
  • The surviving components are the ones you actually integrate.

Room 6: Integrate Over the Whole Object

  • Add all differential contributions:
  • $$\vec E=\int d\vec E.$$
  • The integration variable depends on the shape: length, angle, area, radius, or volume.

Room 7: Link to the Rest of Chapter 22

  • Ring page uses $$dq=\lambda ds.$$
  • Disk page uses $$dq=\sigma(2\pi r\,dr)$$ by slicing into rings.
  • Finite rod page uses $$dq=\lambda dx.$$
Detailed Notes

The Real Setup Pattern

Most continuous-charge problems are won before the integral starts. If you choose the correct tiny element and write the correct expression for $$dq,$$ the rest becomes geometry plus algebra rather than guesswork.

Fast Diagnostic

If you are stuck, ask three questions in order: what tiny element am I using, what is $$dq$$ for that element, and which components survive by symmetry? That sequence fixes most setup errors immediately.
