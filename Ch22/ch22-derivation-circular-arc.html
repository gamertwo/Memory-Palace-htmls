Derivation

Electric Field at the Center of a 120 Degree Charged Arc

This derivation feels different from the ring and disk because the source is only part of a circle, not the whole thing. That means cancellation is partial, not complete. The good news is that opposite angular elements still cancel their vertical pieces, so one horizontal component survives and integrates cleanly across the visible arc.

$$\vec E=\frac{0.83Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\,\hat{\imath}\quad\text{(toward the arc)}$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Source
Uniform 120 degree arc of radius $$r$$ and total charge $$Q.$$
Slice
Use angular element $$dq=\lambda r\,d\theta.$$
Symmetry
Vertical components cancel pairwise; horizontal parts add.
Direction
The field points toward the arc along the symmetry axis.
Rooms: Full Derivation

Room 1: Model the Arc with Tiny Angular Pieces

  • Take one tiny source element on the arc.
  • Because the source lies on a circle of radius $$r,$$ its arc length is $$ds=r\,d\theta.$$
  • So $$dq=\lambda ds=\lambda r\,d\theta.$$

Why This Step

The arc is a one-dimensional curved source, so the natural variable is angle, not x-position. Writing $$dq$$ in angular form matches the object's geometry perfectly.

Room 2: Write the Differential Field Magnitude

  • Every element sits the same distance $$r$$ from the center.
  • So $$dE=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{dq}{r^2}=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\lambda\,d\theta}{r}.$$
  • The distance simplicity is what makes the arc integral manageable.

Why This Step

This mirrors the ring derivation: constant radius means constant distance to the center. That lets the field magnitude simplify before we even touch symmetry.

Room 3: Keep Only the Surviving Component

  • Pair elements at angles $$+\theta$$ and $$-\theta.$$
  • Their vertical components cancel, while their horizontal components point in the same direction.
  • So $$dE_x=dE\cos\theta.$$

Why This Step

The arc is not a full circle, so cancellation is incomplete. Symmetry still helps, but now it removes only one component rather than the whole field.

Room 4: Integrate Across the 120 Degree Span

  • The arc runs from $$-60^\circ$$ to $$+60^\circ.$$
  • So $$E_x=\dfrac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\cos\theta\,d\theta.$$
  • This becomes $$E_x=\dfrac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\left[\sin\theta\right]_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}= \dfrac{1.73\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}.$$

Why This Step

Once the correct component is chosen, the angular integral is short and direct. The hard thinking was really in the geometry and symmetry, not the calculus.

Room 5: Replace $$\lambda$$ with Total Charge

  • The arc length is one-third of a full circumference: $$L=2\pi r/3.$$
  • So $$\lambda=Q/L=3Q/(2\pi r).$$
  • Substituting gives $$E\approx \dfrac{0.83Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}.$$

Why This Step

Rewriting in terms of total charge makes the answer easier to interpret and compare with other Chapter 22 results. It also removes the extra density symbol from the final memory target.
