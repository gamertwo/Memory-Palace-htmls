Potential of a Dipole and a Continuous Charge Distribution

This page shows the next level of scalar advantage. First, use two point-charge potentials to build the far-field dipole potential. Then replace the discrete sum by an integral for a continuous charge distribution, where the basic differential piece is $$dV=k\,dq/r.$$

$$V_{\text{dipole}}\approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2},\qquad V=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{dq}{r}$$

Rooms: Core Idea

Room 1: Build Dipole Potential from Two Point Charges

  • A dipole is still just $$+q$$ and $$-q$$ separated by distance $$d.$$
  • So the exact potential comes from adding the two point-charge potentials algebraically.
  • The starting move is still scalar superposition.

Why This Step

This prevents the dipole formula from becoming something mysterious. It is simply the next layer built from point-charge potential.

Room 2: Use the Far-Field Dipole Form

  • Far from the dipole, the exact sum simplifies.
  • The result becomes $$V\approx \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}.$$
  • This depends on orientation through $$\cos\theta,$$ unlike the field magnitude for a single point charge.

Why This Step

The far-field limit isolates the signature dipole behavior and shows how angular dependence enters the scalar potential in a clean way.

Room 3: Replace Sums by Integrals for Continuous Charge

  • When the source charge is spread continuously, we no longer sum over discrete charges.
  • Instead, we write one differential piece: $$dV=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{dq}{r}.$$
  • Then we integrate over the entire source.

Why This Step

This is the continuous-charge version of scalar potential thinking. Because potential is scalar, there are no vector components to track inside the integral.

Room 4: See Why Potential Integrals Are Often Easier Than Field Integrals

  • The potential integral uses $$1/r,$$ not a vector component structure.
  • There is no cancellation bookkeeping inside the integrand.
  • This is why line and disk potentials can be easier than their field counterparts.

Why This Step

This is one of the biggest practical takeaways of the chapter. Potential can be the easier route even when the final goal is the electric field.
