Reverse Direction

Calculating the Electric Field from the Potential

This page closes the loop of Chapter 24. Earlier pages turned field into potential; now we go backward. The key idea is that the electric field points in the direction of greatest potential decrease, and its magnitude is the rate at which potential drops with distance.

$$E=-\frac{dV}{ds},\qquad E_x=-\frac{dV}{dx}$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Rooms: Core Idea

Room 1: Start from the Potential-Difference Formula

  • For a tiny displacement $$d\vec s,$$ the potential change is $$dV=-\vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
  • If the displacement is along the field direction, this becomes $$dV=-E\,ds.$$
  • So $$E=-dV/ds.$$

Why This Step

This is just the infinitesimal version of the earlier line-integral formula. Shrinking the move down to a tiny step reveals the local field-potential relationship.

Room 2: Read the Minus Sign Correctly

  • The field points toward decreasing potential.
  • Where potential drops faster, the field is stronger.
  • So the minus sign carries the physical direction information.

Why This Step

Students often treat the minus sign as formal algebra only. Here it has a direct physical meaning: fields push positive test charges downhill in potential.

Room 3: Connect with Equipotential Surfaces

  • Equipotential surfaces are surfaces of constant $$V.$$
  • Moving along one of them gives no change in potential and no work by the field.
  • The field must therefore be perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.

Why This Step

This turns the derivative formula into geometry. The field is not just related to numbers in a formula; it is visually encoded by how tightly packed the equipotential surfaces are.

Room 4: Use the One-Dimensional Shortcut

  • When the potential varies only with x, use $$E_x=-dV/dx.$$
  • This is often how textbook examples report the field from a known potential graph or formula.
  • The steeper the graph, the larger the field magnitude.

Why This Step

This shortcut makes Chapter 24 practical. It lets you recover the field from scalar potential data without redoing force calculations.
