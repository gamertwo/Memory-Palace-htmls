Calculating Potential Difference from the Field

This page shows the main bridge from field to potential: if you know the electric field along a path, you can find the potential difference by integrating $$-\vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$ In a uniform field, this collapses to the famous shortcut $$\Delta V=-Ed$$ along the field direction.

$$V_f-V_i=-\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s,\qquad \Delta V=-Ed\text{ for a uniform field along the path}$$

Rooms: Full Derivation

Room 1: Start with Work Done by the Field

  • For a small displacement $$d\vec s,$$ the differential work is $$dW=\vec F\cdot d\vec s.$$
  • For a test charge $$q_0,$$ the force is $$\vec F=q_0\vec E.$$
  • So $$dW=q_0\vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$

Why This Step

Potential difference is tied to work, so we begin at the work level before dividing by charge. This keeps the physical meaning clear throughout the derivation.

Room 2: Integrate Along the Path

  • Add all the tiny work pieces from the initial point to the final point.
  • This gives $$W=q_0\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
  • Then divide by $$q_0$$ and attach the minus sign from the potential-difference definition.

Why This Step

Potential difference depends on the total work per unit charge over the whole move, not on one local force value alone.

Room 3: Write the General Potential-Difference Formula

  • The result is $$V_f-V_i=-\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
  • This is the master formula for turning field information into potential information.
  • The minus sign means potential drops in the direction the field does positive work.

Why This Step

This formula is the Chapter 24 analog of Gauss's law: a general relation that becomes especially useful in high-symmetry or uniform-field situations.

Room 4: Apply It to a Uniform Field Along a Straight Path

  • If $$\vec E$$ is constant and parallel to the displacement, then $$\vec E\cdot d\vec s=E\,ds.$$
  • The integral becomes $$V_f-V_i=-E\int_i^f ds=-Ed.$$
  • So moving along the field direction lowers the potential.

Why This Step

The uniform-field case is the practical shortcut students use most often. It is just the general integral with all the difficult variation removed.
