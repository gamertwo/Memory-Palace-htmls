Reducible to Homogeneous - Question 1

This equation looks close to homogeneous, but it is not directly usable with $y=vx$ because the constants $-20$ and $-10$ spoil the ratio pattern. The clean way is to translate the variables first, so the linear expressions become pure first-degree forms in the new coordinates, and only then apply the homogeneous substitution.

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2x+9y-20}{6x+2y-10}$$
Method Preview

Why Direct $$y=vx$$ Fails

If you try $y=vx$ immediately, the constants remain in the numerator and denominator, so the equation does not reduce to a function of $v$ alone. That means it is not directly homogeneous in the original variables.

What the Shift Does

We move the origin to the point where both linear expressions become zero. After that shift, the constants disappear and the equation becomes homogeneous in the new variables $X$ and $Y$.

What Success Looks Like

Once the equation becomes a ratio of degree-1 forms, the substitution $Y=vX$ works exactly like an ordinary homogeneous differential equation, and the rest becomes separation plus back-substitution.

Rooms

Room 1: Translate Variables to Kill Constants

The real obstacle is the constant part of the two linear expressions. So instead of attacking the differential equation directly, we first shift the coordinate system to a better origin.

  • Set a translated coordinate system:
  • $$x=X+h,\qquad y=Y+k.$$
  • Now rewrite the numerator:
  • $$2x+9y-20=2(X+h)+9(Y+k)-20=(2X+9Y)+(2h+9k-20).$$
  • Rewrite the denominator too:
  • $$6x+2y-10=6(X+h)+2(Y+k)-10=(6X+2Y)+(6h+2k-10).$$
  • To make the new equation homogeneous, force both constant brackets to become zero:
  • $$2h+9k-20=0,\qquad 6h+2k-10=0.$$
  • Solving gives $$h=1,\qquad k=2.$$
  • So the useful translated variables are
  • $$X=x-1,\qquad Y=y-2.$$
  • Because the shift is by constants only, derivatives do not change:
  • $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dY}{dX}.$$
Checkpoint: the shift is chosen so that the intersection point of the two lines $$2x+9y-20=0$$ and $$6x+2y-10=0$$ becomes the new origin. That is the geometric reason the constants disappear.

Room 2: Reduced Homogeneous Equation

Now substitute the translated variables into the differential equation. This is the payoff for Room 1: the constant clutter is gone.

  • After substitution, the equation becomes
  • $$\frac{dY}{dX}=\frac{2X+9Y}{6X+2Y}.$$
  • Both numerator and denominator are first-degree expressions in $$X$$ and $$Y.$$
  • That means the right side depends only on the ratio $$Y/X,$$ so the equation is now homogeneous.
Why this matters: now the standard homogeneous substitution is legal. Before translation it was not.

Room 3: Put $$Y=vX$$

This is the standard homogeneous move. Once the right side is a function of $$Y/X$$, write $$Y=vX$$ and convert the derivative.

  • Use
  • $$Y=vX \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{dY}{dX}=v+X\frac{dv}{dX}.$$
  • Also $$\dfrac{Y}{X}=v,$$ so the right side becomes
  • $$\frac{2X+9Y}{6X+2Y}=\frac{2+9v}{6+2v}.$$
  • Hence
  • $$v+X\frac{dv}{dX}=\frac{2+9v}{6+2v}.$$
  • Now isolate the derivative term:
  • $$X\frac{dv}{dX}=\frac{2+9v-v(6+2v)}{6+2v}=\frac{2+3v-2v^2}{6+2v}.$$
  • Factor numerator and denominator:
  • $$X\frac{dv}{dX}=\frac{(2-v)(2v+1)}{2(v+3)}.$$
Checkpoint: the whole purpose of $$Y=vX$$ is to turn a two-variable equation into a one-variable equation in $$v$$, with $$X$$ appearing only through $$dX/X$$.

Room 4: Separate and Integrate

Once the equation is in $$v$$ and $$X$$, it becomes an ordinary separable equation. This room is just algebra plus controlled integration.

  • Separate the variables:
  • $$\frac{2(v+3)}{(2-v)(2v+1)}\,dv=\frac{dX}{X}.$$
  • Now split the left side into partial fractions:
  • $$\frac{2(v+3)}{(2-v)(2v+1)}=\frac{A}{2-v}+\frac{B}{2v+1}.$$
  • Solving gives $$A=2,\quad B=2,$$ so
  • $$\frac{2(v+3)}{(2-v)(2v+1)}=\frac{2}{2-v}+\frac{2}{2v+1}.$$
  • Integrate term by term:
  • $$\int \frac{2}{2-v}\,dv+\int \frac{2}{2v+1}\,dv=\int \frac{dX}{X}.$$
  • $$-2\ln|2-v|+\ln|2v+1|=\ln|X|+C.$$
  • Combine the logarithms into one expression:
  • $$\ln\left|\frac{2v+1}{(2-v)^2}\right|=\ln|X|+C.$$
Anti-mistake check: the first log has a negative sign because $$\int \frac{1}{2-v}\,dv=-\ln|2-v|$$, not $$+\ln|2-v|$$.

Room 5: Back-Substitute

The hard part is over. Now convert the logarithmic relation into algebra, then undo both substitutions in the correct order: first $$v\to Y/X$$, then $$X,Y\to x,y$$.

  • Exponentiate the logarithmic equation:
  • $$\frac{2v+1}{(2-v)^2}=CX.$$
  • Now put $$v=\dfrac{Y}{X}:$$
  • $$\frac{2\left(\frac{Y}{X}\right)+1}{\left(2-\frac{Y}{X}\right)^2}=CX.$$
  • Simplify numerator and denominator carefully:
  • $$\frac{\frac{2Y+X}{X}}{\frac{(2X-Y)^2}{X^2}}=CX.$$
  • That gives
  • $$\frac{X(2Y+X)}{(2X-Y)^2}=CX.$$
  • Cancel the common factor $$X$$ into the constant:
  • $$\frac{2Y+X}{(2X-Y)^2}=C.$$
  • So the reduced-variable answer is
  • $$\boxed{(2X-Y)^2=C(2Y+X)}.$$
  • Now return to the original variables:
  • $$2X-Y=2(x-1)-(y-2)=2x-y,$$
  • $$2Y+X=2(y-2)+(x-1)=x+2y-5.$$
  • Therefore
  • $$\boxed{(2x-y)^2=C(x+2y-5)}.$$
Final logic: the answer still contains the effect of both substitutions. If you skip the translation back from $$X,Y$$ to $$x,y$$, you stop one step too early.
Final Answer

Using translation + homogeneous substitution, the required solution is $$\boxed{(2x-y)^2=C(x+2y-5)}.$$ The important idea is not just the algebra: this problem teaches that when constants stop an equation from being directly homogeneous, you should first shift the origin to the right point and only then use the standard homogeneous method.

Quick Recognition Rule

If numerator and denominator are linear in $$x,y$$ but include constants, test whether a translation $$x=X+h,\ y=Y+k$$ can remove those constants. That is the standard trigger for the reducible-to-homogeneous method.

Most Common Mistake

Students often jump to $$y=vx$$ too early. That fails here because the constants do not disappear until after the shift. The translation is not optional; it is the enabling step.

Fast Verification Idea

You can verify the final form by differentiating implicitly, but the cleaner check is conceptual: the shifted equation is homogeneous, the substitution is valid, and every reverse substitution was handled consistently.
