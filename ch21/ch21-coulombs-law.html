Section 21-4

Coulomb's Law

This page locks the electrostatic force rule. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract, and the force magnitude depends on the product of the charges divided by the square of the separation. The writing rule is simple: decide direction from the signs first, then compute magnitude from Coulomb's law.

$$F=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{|q_1||q_2|}{r^2},\qquad i=\frac{dq}{dt}$$

Quick Data
Direction Rule
Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract.
Coulomb Unit
From $$i=\dfrac{dq}{dt},$$ one coulomb equals one ampere-second.
Vector Form
$$\vec F = k\dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\hat r$$ with the geometry deciding the direction.
Rooms: Core Law

Room 1: Draw the Force on the Particle That Feels It

  • Every electrostatic force vector must have its tail on the particle that feels the force.
  • This prevents one of the easiest diagram mistakes in the chapter.
  • Start by naming the particle of interest before you sketch arrows.

Room 2: Use Charge Signs to Decide Pull or Push

  • Two positive charges repel, and two negative charges repel.
  • A positive and a negative charge attract.
  • Direction is a geometry decision, not a late algebra fix.

Room 3: Compute the Magnitude with Coulomb's Law

  • The electrostatic force magnitude is $$F=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{|q_1||q_2|}{r^2}.$$
  • Use magnitudes in the formula, then attach the direction from the diagram.
  • The inverse-square form means distance changes the force quickly.

Room 4: Connect Charge to Current

  • The SI unit of charge is the coulomb.
  • Because $$i=\dfrac{dq}{dt},$$ one coulomb is the charge transported by one ampere in one second.
  • This gives the unit a physical meaning instead of making it feel arbitrary.

Key Shortcut

Direction first, magnitude second. If you reverse that order, Coulomb problems get confusing fast.
