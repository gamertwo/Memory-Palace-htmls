Exercise 8-24

Use Superposition When the Output Terms Live at Different Frequencies

This exercise is the voltage-output version of Example 8-17. The sources drive the circuit at different angular frequencies, so the two output responses must be found separately and written back as separate time-domain sinusoids. The memory emphasis is that different frequencies do not collapse into one phasor output, even when the circuit itself stays the same.

$$v_o(t)=-7.07\cos(10{,}000t-45^\circ)+8.94\cos(20{,}000t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}$$
Major Guide Open in Memory Builder
What You Are Asked To Find
Given
Use superposition for Figure 8-36 when $$i_s(t)=100\cos(10{,}000t)\text{ mA}$$ and $$v_s(t)=20\cos(20{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}.$$
Find
Find the output voltage $$v_o(t).$$
Rule
Different frequencies mean separate partial waveforms, not one final phasor.
Quick Data
Source 1 Response
$$v_{o1}(t)=-7.07\cos(10{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}$$
Source 2 Response
$$v_{o2}(t)=8.94\cos(20{,}000t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}$$
Final Output
$$v_o(t)=v_{o1}(t)+v_{o2}(t)$$
How To Convert A Sinusoid To Phasor

Use the standard cosine-form rule:

$$A\cos(\omega t+\theta)\ \longrightarrow\ A\angle\theta$$
Current Source
$$i_s(t)=100\cos(10{,}000t)\text{ mA}$$ Compare with $$A\cos(\omega t+\theta).$$

Amplitude $$=100\text{ mA}=0.1\text{ A}$$ and phase $$=0^\circ.$$

Therefore: $$I_s=0.1\angle 0^\circ\text{ A}$$
Voltage Source
$$v_s(t)=20\cos(20{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}$$ Amplitude $$=20$$ and phase $$=-45^\circ.$$

Therefore: $$V_s=20\angle -45^\circ\text{ V}$$
What Disappears?
The term $$\omega t$$ is not written inside the phasor. The phasor stores only the magnitude and phase. Frequency is still used later when computing: $$Z_L=j\omega L,\qquad Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C}$$
First Contribution: Current Source Only

To find the first contribution, turn off the voltage source. A turned-off voltage source becomes a short circuit. Then solve the reduced circuit at the current-source frequency.

Step 1: Keep Only The Current Source

Given: $$i_s(t)=100\cos(10{,}000t)\text{ mA}$$ Convert to phasor: $$I_s=0.1\angle 0^\circ\text{ A}$$

Step 2: Use The Frequency Of This Source

For the first reduced circuit: $$\omega_1=10{,}000\text{ rad/s}$$

Step 3: Convert The Reactive Elements To Impedance

Inductor: $$Z_L=j\omega L=j(10{,}000)(0.2)=j2000\ \Omega$$ Capacitor: $$Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C} =\frac{1}{j(10{,}000)(0.1\mu F)} =-j1000\ \Omega$$

Step 4: Where Does The Phase Come From?

The source phasor is $$I_s=0.1\angle 0^\circ$$ so the source itself contributes no phase shift.

Therefore the phase in the output must come from the reduced impedance network. In other words: $$V_{o1}=I_s\cdot Z_{\text{transfer}}$$ and the angle of $$V_{o1}$$ comes from the angle of $$Z_{\text{transfer}}.$$

Step 5: Where Does The Magnitude 7.07 Come From?

The textbook result is: $$V_{o1}=-7.07\angle -45^\circ\text{ V}$$ Since $$V_{o1}=I_s\cdot Z_{\text{transfer}}$$ we compare magnitudes: $$|V_{o1}|=|I_s|\cdot |Z_{\text{transfer}}|$$ $$7.07=(0.1)|Z_{\text{transfer}}|$$ so: $$|Z_{\text{transfer}}|=\frac{7.07}{0.1}=70.7\ \Omega$$ Hence the circuit transfer magnitude is $$70.7\ \Omega,$$ and: $$0.1\times 70.7=7.07$$

Step 6: Why Is The Angle -45°?

Since the source angle is $$0^\circ,$$ the output angle comes entirely from the circuit: $$0^\circ + (-45^\circ)=-45^\circ$$ So the reduced network behaves like a transfer impedance with angle $$-45^\circ.$$

Step 7: First Phasor Contribution

Therefore: $$V_{o1}=-7.07\angle -45^\circ\text{ V}$$ and back in the time domain: $$v_{o1}(t)=-7.07\cos(10{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}$$

$$I_s=0.1\angle0^\circ,\quad Z_L=j2000,\quad Z_C=-j1000,\quad V_{o1}=-7.07\angle-45^\circ$$
Second Contribution: Voltage Source Only

To find the second contribution, turn off the current source. A turned-off current source becomes an open circuit. Then solve the reduced circuit at the voltage-source frequency.

Step 1: Keep Only The Voltage Source

Given: $$v_s(t)=20\cos(20{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}$$ Convert to phasor: $$V_s=20\angle -45^\circ\text{ V}$$

Step 2: Use The Frequency Of This Source

For the second reduced circuit: $$\omega_2=20{,}000\text{ rad/s}$$

Step 3: Convert The Reactive Elements To Impedance

Inductor: $$Z_L=j\omega L=j(20{,}000)(0.2)=j4000\ \Omega$$ Capacitor: $$Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C} =\frac{1}{j(20{,}000)(0.1\mu F)} =-j500\ \Omega$$

Step 4: Where Does 8.94 Come From?

The textbook result is: $$V_{o2}=8.94\angle -18.4^\circ\text{ V}$$ Since this time the source is a voltage source, the reduced circuit acts through a voltage transfer ratio: $$V_{o2}=V_s\cdot H(j\omega)$$ Compare magnitudes: $$|V_{o2}|=|V_s|\cdot |H|$$ $$8.94=20\cdot |H|$$ so: $$|H|=\frac{8.94}{20}=0.447$$ Therefore the circuit at $$\omega=20{,}000$$ passes about $$44.7\%$$ of the source magnitude to the output: $$20\times 0.447=8.94$$

Step 5: Where Does The Angle -18.4° Come From?

The source already begins with phase: $$-45^\circ$$ and the reduced circuit adds its own phase shift. Since the final answer is: $$V_{o2}=8.94\angle -18.4^\circ$$ the network must contribute: $$-18.4^\circ-(-45^\circ)=+26.6^\circ$$ So the transfer ratio behaves like: $$H(j\omega)\approx 0.447\angle 26.6^\circ$$

Step 6: Second Phasor Contribution

Therefore: $$V_{o2}=8.94\angle -18.4^\circ\text{ V}$$ and back in the time domain: $$v_{o2}(t)=8.94\cos(20{,}000t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}$$

$$V_s=20\angle-45^\circ,\quad Z_L=j4000,\quad Z_C=-j500,\quad V_{o2}=8.94\angle-18.4^\circ$$
Why The Two Contributions Cannot Be Combined As One Phasor
First Term
$$v_{o1}(t)=-7.07\cos(10{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}$$
Second Term
$$v_{o2}(t)=8.94\cos(20{,}000t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}$$
Reason
One term lives at $$10{,}000\text{ rad/s}$$ and the other lives at $$20{,}000\text{ rad/s}.$$ Since phasors only combine when they share the same frequency, these must be added in the time domain: $$v_o(t)=v_{o1}(t)+v_{o2}(t)$$
Compact Exam-Style Working

First contribution:

$$i_s(t)=100\cos(10^4 t)\text{ mA}\Rightarrow I_s=0.1\angle0^\circ\text{ A}$$ $$\omega=10^4,\quad Z_L=j2000,\quad Z_C=-j1000$$ $$V_{o1}=-7.07\angle-45^\circ\text{ V}$$ $$v_{o1}(t)=-7.07\cos(10^4 t-45^\circ)\text{ V}$$

Second contribution:

$$v_s(t)=20\cos(2\times10^4 t-45^\circ)\Rightarrow V_s=20\angle-45^\circ\text{ V}$$ $$\omega=2\times10^4,\quad Z_L=j4000,\quad Z_C=-j500$$ $$V_{o2}=8.94\angle-18.4^\circ\text{ V}$$ $$v_{o2}(t)=8.94\cos(2\times10^4 t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}$$

Final answer:

$$v_o(t)=-7.07\cos(10^4 t-45^\circ)+8.94\cos(2\times10^4 t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}$$
Rooms: Full Solution

Room 1: Recognize the Different-Frequency Superposition Rule

  • The current source and the voltage source do not share one common angular frequency.
  • So the output must be built from two separate steady-state responses.
  • The final answer will be a time-domain sum of two cosine terms.

Room 2: Find the Output Due to the Current Source Alone

  • Turn off the voltage source and analyze the circuit at $$\omega_1=10{,}000\text{ rad/s}.$$
  • The resulting output term given by the textbook is $$v_{o1}(t)=-7.07\cos(10{,}000t-45^\circ)\text{ V}.$$

Room 3: Find the Output Due to the Voltage Source Alone

  • Now turn off the current source and analyze the circuit at $$\omega_2=20{,}000\text{ rad/s}.$$
  • The second output term is $$v_{o2}(t)=8.94\cos(20{,}000t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}.$$

Room 4: Add the Outputs in the Time Domain

  • Do not try to combine the two responses into one phasor. Their frequencies are different.
  • Simply add the two time-domain pieces:
  • $$v_o(t)=v_{o1}(t)+v_{o2}(t).$$

Room 5: State the Final Textbook Answer

  • The final answer is
  • $$v_o(t)=-7.07\cos(10{,}000t-45^\circ)+8.94\cos(20{,}000t-18.4^\circ)\text{ V}.$$
  • The memory hook is: one source, one frequency, one waveform term.

Key Shortcut

Different-frequency superposition never ends with one phasor. It ends with one cosine term per source.

Why This Flow Matters

This page closes the superposition sequence by making the output type irrelevant and the frequency rule central. Once the frequency test is clear, the finishing rule becomes automatic.

Solution Flow

Use the two-frequency superposition route: isolate one source, write one waveform, repeat, then add the time-domain pieces.
