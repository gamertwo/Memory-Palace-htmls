Section 8-1: Sinusoids and Phasors

This page turns the textbook section into a step-by-step review path: what a phasor means, how Euler's identity connects the time domain to the complex plane, and how polar and rectangular phasor forms say the same thing.

$$v(t)=V_A\cos(\omega t+\phi)=\Re\{V_Ae^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}\}=\Re\{Ve^{j\omega t}\}$$
Quick Data
Definition
A phasor is a complex number that stores amplitude and phase.
Convention
This chapter uses cosine as the reference waveform.
Key Link
Euler's relation connects sinusoidal functions to complex exponentials.
Important Limit
The phasor itself does not contain the signal frequency.
Rooms

Room 1: Start With Euler's Relation

  • Euler's identity is $$e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta.$$
  • So the cosine and sine functions can be read from a complex exponential using real and imaginary parts.
  • That is why sinusoidal steady-state analysis can be moved into complex-number form.

Room 2: Rewrite the Sinusoid

  • For $$v(t)=V_A\cos(\omega t+\phi),$$ write the cosine as the real part of a complex exponential.
  • Then $$v(t)=\Re\{V_Ae^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}=\Re\{V_Ae^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}\}.$$
  • This separates the time-varying factor from the constant amplitude-phase factor.

Room 3: Define the Phasor

  • By definition, the phasor is the constant complex factor multiplying $$e^{j\omega t}.$$
  • So $$V=V_Ae^{j\phi}=V_A\angle\phi.$$
  • The waveform is then recovered from $$v(t)=\Re\{Ve^{j\omega t}\}.$$

Room 4: Polar and Rectangular Forms

  • Polar form is $$V=V_A\angle\phi.$$
  • Rectangular form is $$V=V_A\cos\phi+jV_A\sin\phi.$$
  • Both forms represent the same complex number, so use whichever makes the next step easier.

Room 5: What the Phasor Keeps and What It Leaves Out

  • The phasor stores amplitude and phase angle.
  • The rotating factor $$e^{j\omega t}$$ carries the frequency information outside the phasor.
  • That only works because, in steady-state, all signals being compared share the same angular frequency.

Room 6: Geometric Picture

  • You can view $$Ve^{j\omega t}$$ as a vector rotating counterclockwise at angular speed $$\omega.$$
  • The actual cosine waveform is the projection of that rotating vector onto the real axis.
  • The phasor $$V$$ is the snapshot of the rotating vector at $$t=0.$$
