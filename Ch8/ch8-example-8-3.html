Example 8-3

Use the Derivative Property

This page trains the derivative reflex: catch the original cosine, write its phasor, let the chalk write $$j\omega$$ across it, and only then coat the derivative back into the time domain.

$$v(t)=15\cos(200t-30^\circ)\text{ V}\qquad \text{Find } \frac{dv(t)}{dt}$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Original Phasor
$$V=15\angle-30^\circ$$
Derivative Rule
$$\dfrac{d}{dt}\longleftrightarrow j\omega$$
Angular Frequency
$$\omega=200$$
Rooms: Full Solution

Room 1: Catch the Original Wave

  • Read the cosine directly into phasor form: $$V=15\angle-30^\circ.$$
  • The amplitude $$15$$ becomes the phasor magnitude, and the phase $$-30^\circ$$ becomes the phasor angle.
  • Keep the same angular frequency $$\omega=200.$$ The derivative changes amplitude and phase, but not the base frequency term inside the cosine.

Room 2: Write the Chalk Factor

  • The derivative property says that differentiating in the time domain corresponds to multiplying by $$j\omega$$ in the phasor domain.
  • So the derivative phasor is $$j\omega V=(j200)(15\angle-30^\circ).$$
  • This one factor does two jobs at once: $$\omega$$ changes the magnitude, while $$j$$ rotates the angle.

Room 3: Combine Magnitude and Angle

  • Rewrite the chalk factor in polar form. Because $$j$$ is a $$90^\circ$$ rotation, $$j200=200\angle90^\circ.$$
  • Now multiply in polar form by multiplying magnitudes and adding angles:
  • $$j\omega V=200\angle90^\circ\cdot15\angle-30^\circ.$$
  • The magnitude becomes $$200\times15=3000,$$ and the angle becomes $$90^\circ+(-30^\circ)=60^\circ.$$
  • So the derivative phasor is $$3000\angle60^\circ.$$

Room 4: Build the Derivative Waveform

  • The derivative keeps the same angular frequency $$\omega=200.$$
  • Now coat the phasor back into the time domain with $$M\angle\theta\rightarrow M\cos(\omega t+\theta).$$
  • That means amplitude $$3000,$$ frequency term $$200t,$$ and phase shift $$+60^\circ.$$
  • So $$\frac{dv(t)}{dt}=3000\cos(200t+60^\circ)\text{ V/s}.$$

Room 5: Lock the Pattern

  • Differentiation in the time domain becomes multiplication by $$j\omega$$ in the phasor domain.
  • That means magnitude is multiplied by $$\omega$$ and angle increases by $$90^\circ.$$
  • So for any cosine phasor, the derivative rule is: stretch by $$\omega,$$ rotate forward by $$90^\circ,$$ then coat back if the question wants a waveform.

Key Shortcut

For derivatives, think "multiply the length by $$\omega$$ and rotate the arrow by $$+90^\circ$$" before you coat the final cosine.

Why $$j\omega$$ Does Both Jobs

The factor $$\omega$$ changes the magnitude because differentiation scales the amplitude by angular frequency. The factor $$j$$ changes the angle because multiplying by $$j$$ is a quarter-turn in the phasor plane. So the derivative property is really two actions happening together: stretch by $$\omega$$ and rotate by $$90^\circ$$.

Why The Frequency Stays The Same

Differentiation does not change the underlying oscillation rate. The signal still swings with the same $$\omega,$$ but its amplitude and phase shift change. That is why the final waveform keeps $$200t$$ even though the coefficient in front becomes much larger.
