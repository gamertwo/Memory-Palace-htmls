Exercise 8-6

Convert a Mixed Cosine-Sine Waveform to One Phasor

This exercise trains the last Chapter 8 bridge: standardize the sine term into cosine-reference form, read each piece as a phasor, add in rectangular form, and box the one clean result.

$$v(t)=V_A\cos(\omega t)+2V_A\sin(\omega t)$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Sine Bridge
$$\sin(\omega t)=\cos(\omega t-90^\circ)$$
Rectangular Sum
$$V=V_A-j2V_A$$
Answer
$$V=\sqrt{5}\,V_A\angle-63.4^\circ$$
Rooms: Full Solution

Room 1: Standardize the Sine Term

  • Phasor reading is easiest from cosine-reference form, so first rewrite the sine term as a cosine with a phase shift.
  • Use $$\sin(\omega t)=\cos(\omega t-90^\circ).$$
  • That turns the second term into $$2V_A\sin(\omega t)=2V_A\cos(\omega t-90^\circ).$$
  • Now both pieces are written in the same cosine language, which means they can be read as phasors consistently.

Room 2: Read Both Phasors

  • The first term reads directly as $$V_A\cos(\omega t)\rightarrow V_A\angle0^\circ=V_A+j0.$$
  • The rewritten sine term reads as $$2V_A\cos(\omega t-90^\circ)\rightarrow2V_A\angle-90^\circ.$$
  • To understand its rectangular form, note that an angle of $$-90^\circ$$ points straight down the imaginary axis, so it has no real part.
  • That is why $$2V_A\angle-90^\circ=-j2V_A.$$

Room 3: Add on the Mat

  • Now add the two rectangular pieces component by component:
  • $$V=V_A+j0-j2V_A=V_A-j2V_A.$$
  • The real part stays $$V_A,$$ while the imaginary part is $$-2V_A.$$
  • This places the phasor in quadrant IV, since the real part is positive and the imaginary part is negative.

Room 4: Rebuild Polar Form

  • Use the rectangular coordinates to find the magnitude: $$|V|=\sqrt{V_A^2+(2V_A)^2}=\sqrt{5}\,V_A.$$
  • Now find the angle: $$\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{-2V_A}{V_A}\right)=\tan^{-1}(-2)=-63.4^\circ.$$
  • The negative angle matches the quadrant-IV picture, so no further angle correction is needed.

Room 5: Box the One Clean Phasor

  • Therefore $$\boxed{V=V_A-j2V_A=\sqrt{5}\,V_A\angle-63.4^\circ\text{ V}}.$$
  • This page ends in phasor form, so the coat is not needed.
  • The whole trick on this page was converting the sine term into cosine-reference form before reading phasors.

Key Shortcut

The sine term is the whole trick. Once you rewrite it as a cosine with a $$-90^\circ$$ phase, the rest is ordinary phasor addition.

Why The Sine Becomes Negative Imaginary

After rewriting $$\sin(\omega t)$$ as $$\cos(\omega t-90^\circ),$$ the phase is $$-90^\circ.$$ In phasor language that points straight down the imaginary axis, so the term contributes no real part and only a negative imaginary part. That is why the second piece becomes $$-j2V_A$$ instead of something with both real and imaginary parts.

Why The Amplitude Becomes $$\sqrt5\,V_A$$

Once the rectangular form is $$V_A-j2V_A,$$ the phasor is just the point $$(V_A,-2V_A).$$ The magnitude is the distance from the origin to that point, so the Pythagorean theorem gives $$\sqrt{V_A^2+(2V_A)^2}=\sqrt5\,V_A.$$
