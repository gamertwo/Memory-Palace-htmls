Design Exercise 8-11

Design a Divider for a Shifted Cosine Output

This design page is now built around one memorization chain: confirm the phasors first, lock the divider ratio, lock the divider formula, rearrange it into a relation between $$Z_1$$ and $$Z_2,$$ convert that relation into polar form so the geometry is memorable, choose a practical $$Z_2$$ and know why, then use $$\omega$$ to pull out the final component values.

$$v_s(t)=50\cos(2000t)\text{ V},\qquad v_o(t)=25\cos(2000t-30^\circ)\text{ V}$$ $$V_O=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}V_S$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Confirmed Phasors
$$V_S=50\angle0^\circ,\qquad V_O=25\angle-30^\circ$$
Target Ratio
$$\frac{V_O}{V_S}=0.5\angle-30^\circ$$
Solved Relation
$$Z_1=(0.7321+j1)Z_2=1.239\angle53.79^\circ Z_2$$
Rooms: Full Solution

Room 1: Confirm the Phasors and the Divider Ratio

  • The source is already in cosine-reference form, so $$V_S=50\angle0^\circ.$$
  • The required output is also already in cosine-reference form, so $$V_O=25\angle-30^\circ.$$
  • Before doing any design algebra, confirm the ratio you want the divider to produce: $$\frac{V_O}{V_S}=\frac{25\angle-30^\circ}{50\angle0^\circ}=0.5\angle-30^\circ.$$
  • This ratio is the design target you must memorize first, because the whole divider equation has to reproduce this exact magnitude and angle.

Room 2: Lock the Divider Formula and Substitute the Ratio

  • The formula to memorize is the divider law: $$V_O=\dfrac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}V_S.$$
  • Divide both sides by $$V_S$$ so the ratio stands alone: $$\frac{V_O}{V_S}=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}.$$
  • Now substitute the confirmed target ratio: $$0.5\angle-30^\circ=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}.$$
  • This is the core design equation. Everything that follows is just rearranging this ratio into a usable relationship between the two impedances.

Room 3: Rearrange for the Relation Between $$Z_1$$ and $$Z_2$$

  • Rearrange the divider equation so $$Z_1$$ stands alone: $$Z_1=\left(\frac{1}{0.5\angle-30^\circ}-1\right)Z_2.$$
  • Now simplify the coefficient carefully. Since $$\frac{1}{0.5\angle-30^\circ}=2\angle30^\circ,$$ convert that polar form into rectangular form: $$2\angle30^\circ=2(\cos30^\circ+j\sin30^\circ)=1.732+j1.$$
  • Subtract 1 from the real part: $$(1.732+j1)-1=0.7321+j1.$$
  • So the rectangular relation is $$Z_1=(0.7321+j1)Z_2.$$
  • Now extend the picture into polar form. The coefficient magnitude is $$\sqrt{0.7321^2+1^2}=1.239,$$ and the angle is $$\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{0.7321}\right)=53.79^\circ.$$
  • So the same design relation in polar form is $$Z_1=1.239\angle53.79^\circ Z_2.$$ This polar version is useful because it tells you instantly that $$Z_1$$ must be larger than $$Z_2$$ and tilted into the inductive quadrant.

Room 4: Choose a Practical $$Z_2$$ and Know Why

  • This equation gives a family of valid solutions, not just one answer, so now you choose a practical $$Z_2.$$
  • A simple and useful choice is $$Z_2=1000\,\Omega.$$ This is nice because it is purely real, easy to implement as a standard resistor, and it keeps the arithmetic simple.
  • Substitute it into the relation: $$Z_1=(0.7321+j1)(1000)=732+j1000\,\Omega.$$
  • That means $$Z_1$$ contains a 732-ohm resistor part and an inductive reactance of $$+1000\,\Omega.$$
  • The reason this is a good choice is that it produces ordinary positive component values and a physically reasonable inductor instead of forcing awkward or nonphysical parts.

Room 5: Use $$\omega$$ to Pull Out the Final Values

  • Because $$\omega=2000,$$ the inductive part $$+j1000$$ means $$\omega L=1000,$$ so $$L=1000/2000=0.5\text{ H}.$$
  • The chosen $$Z_2$$ is purely real, so it is simply a 1-kΩ resistor.
  • The real part of $$Z_1$$ is another resistor: $$R=732\,\Omega.$$
  • So one practical final design is $$Z_2=1000\,\Omega$$ and $$Z_1=732+j1000\,\Omega,$$ implemented by a 1-kΩ resistor on one side and a 732-Ω resistor in series with a 0.5-H inductor on the other.
  • Other choices of $$Z_2$$ are possible, but this one is memorable because it uses a clean resistor for $$Z_2$$ and lets $$\omega$$ pull the inductance directly from $$+j1000.$$

Key Shortcut

First memorize the confirmed phasors, then the target ratio, then the divider law. After that, the design becomes one rearrangement plus one practical choice.

Why The Polar Form of the Relation Helps

The rectangular form $$0.7321+j1$$ is good for multiplying by your chosen $$Z_2,$$ but the polar form $$1.239\angle53.79^\circ$$ is easier to remember visually. It tells you the multiplier is larger than 1 and tilted into the positive imaginary direction, so $$Z_1$$ must have an inductive flavor.

Solution Flow

Walk this as one linked design-palace route from the memory-forum style: confirm the phasors first, light the target ratio, lock the divider formula, stretch the algebra until $$Z_1$$ stands alone, rotate that relation into polar form, choose the most memorable $$Z_2$$ and know why, then let $$\omega$$ pull out the resistor and inductor values.
