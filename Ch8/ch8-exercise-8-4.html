Exercise 8-4

Rotate the Balanced Phasors by $$90^\circ$$

This exercise adds one more reflex: a common rotation does not destroy a zero sum. If every phasor rotates by the same angle, the entire triangle rotates together and still closes.

$$I_A=3.21+j3.83,\qquad I_B=-4.92+j0.87,\qquad I_C=1.71-j4.70$$ $$\text{Rotate each phasor }90^\circ\text{ counterclockwise and test the sum.}$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Rotation Rule
A $$90^\circ$$ counterclockwise rotation multiplies a phasor by $$j.$$
Coordinate Rule
$$(a+jb)\xrightarrow{\times j}-b+ja$$
Big Idea
A common rotation preserves a zero vector sum.
Rooms: Full Solution

Room 1: Recall the Original Zero Sum

  • From Example 8-2, the balanced set already satisfies $$I_A+I_B+I_C=0+j0.$$
  • Now the problem asks what happens if every phasor is rotated by the same $$90^\circ$$ counterclockwise.
  • In phasor language, a $$90^\circ$$ counterclockwise rotation means multiplication by $$j.$$

Room 2: Rotate $$I_A$$

  • Use the coordinate rule that comes from multiplying by $$j:$$ if $$a+jb$$ is multiplied by $$j,$$ the result is $$ja+j^2b=-b+ja.$$
  • That is why the shortcut is $$(a+jb)\mapsto-b+ja.$$
  • Apply it to $$I_A=3.21+j3.83.$$ The old imaginary part $$3.83$$ becomes the new negative real part, and the old real part $$3.21$$ becomes the new positive imaginary part.
  • So $$I_A'=j(3.21+j3.83)=-3.83+j3.21.$$

Room 3: Rotate $$I_B$$ and $$I_C$$

  • Use the same swap rule on the other two phasors.
  • For $$I_B=-4.92+j0.87,$$ the old imaginary part $$0.87$$ becomes the new negative real part, and the old real part $$-4.92$$ becomes the new negative imaginary part. So $$I_B'=-0.87-j4.92.$$
  • For $$I_C=1.71-j4.70,$$ the old imaginary part $$-4.70$$ becomes the new positive real part, and the old real part $$1.71$$ becomes the new positive imaginary part. So $$I_C'=4.70+j1.71.$$

Room 4: Add the Rotated Phasors

  • Now add the rotated rectangular coordinates:
  • $$I_A'+I_B'+I_C'=(-3.83-0.87+4.70)+j(3.21-4.92+1.71).$$
  • The real part again cancels to $$0,$$ and the imaginary part also cancels to $$0.$$
  • So $$\boxed{I_A'+I_B'+I_C'=0+j0.}$$
  • The numbers changed position, but the overall balance did not disappear.

Room 5: State the Principle

  • A common rotation multiplies the whole sum by the same phasor factor: $$I_A'+I_B'+I_C'=j(I_A+I_B+I_C).$$
  • Since the original sum is zero, the rotated sum is $$j(0)=0.$$
  • So a balanced zero-sum set stays balanced after a common rotation.

Key Shortcut

Remember the coordinate swap: multiply by $$j$$ means real becomes negative imaginary-source value and imaginary becomes new positive real-source value.

Why The Zero Sum Survives

All three phasors are rotated by the same operator, so the whole vector picture turns together. Nothing about the balance is broken. In coordinate form that shows up as the same swap rule applied to each phasor, and after the swap the real parts still cancel and the imaginary parts still cancel.

How To Remember The $$j$$ Rotation

Multiplying by $$j$$ does two things at once: it rotates the arrow by $$90^\circ$$ and it swaps the rectangular coordinates with a sign change. The old vertical part becomes the new horizontal part with a minus sign, and the old horizontal part becomes the new vertical part. That is the whole reason the shortcut $$(a+jb)\mapsto-b+ja$$ works.
