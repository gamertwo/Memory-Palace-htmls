Exercise 8-5

Find the Phasor of the Time Derivative

This is the short version of the derivative workflow. The question asks for the derivative phasor only, so you stop at the case and do not coat it back into a waveform.

$$v(t)=100\cos(1000t)\text{ V}\qquad \text{Find the phasor corresponding to }\frac{dv(t)}{dt}$$
Major GuideOpen in Memory Builder
Quick Data
Original Phasor
$$V=100\angle0^\circ$$
Derivative Rule
$$V_{\text{derivative}}=j\omega V$$
Answer Type
Phasor only, so no time-domain coat is needed.
Rooms: Full Solution

Room 1: Catch the Original Cosine

  • $$v(t)=100\cos(1000t)\text{ V}$$ gives the phasor $$V=100\angle0^\circ.$$
  • The amplitude becomes the phasor magnitude, and because there is no explicit phase shift, the angle is $$0^\circ.$$
  • Here $$\omega=1000.$$

Room 2: Write the Chalk Rule

  • The phasor of the derivative is $$j\omega V.$$
  • So $$V_{\text{derivative}}=(j1000)(100\angle0^\circ).$$
  • This tells you immediately that the answer will be scaled by $$1000$$ and rotated by $$+90^\circ.$$

Room 3: Convert the Chalk Factor

  • Write the multiplier in polar form: $$j1000=1000\angle90^\circ.$$
  • The $$1000$$ is the new magnitude multiplier, while the $$90^\circ$$ comes from the quarter-turn created by $$j.$$
  • Therefore $$V_{\text{derivative}}=1000\angle90^\circ\cdot100\angle0^\circ.$$

Room 4: Multiply Magnitude and Angle

  • Now multiply magnitudes and add angles, just as with any polar multiplication.
  • Magnitude: $$1000\times100=10^5.$$
  • Angle: $$90^\circ+0^\circ=90^\circ.$$
  • So $$V_{\text{derivative}}=10^5\angle90^\circ\text{ V/s}.$$
  • The phasor points straight up because the original phasor started on the positive real axis and then rotated by $$90^\circ.$$

Room 5: Stop at the Case

  • The problem asks only for the phasor corresponding to the derivative.
  • So you stop here and do not coat the answer back into a time-domain waveform.
  • The final answer is $$\boxed{10^5\angle90^\circ\text{ V/s}}.$$

Key Shortcut

If the original cosine has zero phase, the derivative phasor simply multiplies the magnitude by $$\omega$$ and lands at $$+90^\circ.$$

Why The Answer Is Straight Up

The original phasor starts on the positive real axis because its phase is $$0^\circ.$$ Multiplying by $$j$$ rotates that arrow one quarter-turn counterclockwise, so the derivative phasor points straight up at $$90^\circ.$$ The factor $$1000$$ only stretches the arrow; it does not change that direction.

Why No Rectangular Form Is Needed

This problem is pure polar multiplication, so rectangular form would only slow you down. The derivative rule already comes as a polar-style change: multiply the length by $$\omega$$ and add $$90^\circ$$ to the angle. That makes this page a good speed-drill for the derivative property.
