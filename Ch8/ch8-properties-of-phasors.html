Properties of Phasors

This page isolates the conceptual rules before the examples start: what a phasor represents, why the cosine reference matters, and why same-frequency signals are the only ones you can combine directly in one phasor equation.

$$V=V_Ae^{j\phi}=V_A\angle\phi=V_A\cos\phi+jV_A\sin\phi$$
Quick Data
Keeps
Amplitude and phase.
Omits
Frequency, because frequency is common to the whole steady-state problem.
Needed
A single frequency reference for all combined signals.
Best Use
Algebra on sinusoids becomes complex-number algebra.
Rooms

Room 1: The Meaning of the Phasor

  • The waveform and the phasor are related, but they are not the same object.
  • The waveform lives in time. The phasor is the constant complex label attached to that waveform.
  • So when you see $$V=12\angle -20^\circ,$$ that is not yet the full sinusoid until $$e^{j\omega t}$$ is restored.

Room 2: Same Frequency Is Required

  • Phasor addition works only when all sinusoids have the same $$\omega.$$
  • If two signals rotate at different angular frequencies, they cannot share one common $$e^{j\omega t}$$ factor.
  • That is why the chapter always states a common frequency before adding phasors.

Room 3: Why Frequency Is Not Inside the Phasor

  • The full signal is $$v(t)=\Re\{Ve^{j\omega t}\}.$$
  • The factor $$e^{j\omega t}$$ already contains the frequency.
  • So the phasor only needs amplitude and phase angle.

Room 4: Choose the Convenient Form

  • Use polar form when reading magnitude and phase quickly.
  • Use rectangular form when adding or subtracting phasors.
  • Convert back to polar when you want the final sinusoid amplitude and angle.
