23-10 | Sample Problem: Dipole Approximation

Electric Dipole and Atmospheric Sprites

This page turns a lightning-cloud system into a vertical electric dipole. Once the thunderstorm is modeled as charges separated by a distance $$2h$$, the field high above the cloud becomes a standard axial-dipole problem, and the main lesson is how quickly that field drops with altitude.

$$E(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q(2h)}{z^3}=\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qh}{z^3},\qquad E(30\,\text{km})=1.6\times10^3\,\text{N/C},\qquad E(60\,\text{km})=2.0\times10^2\,\text{N/C}$$

What To Notice

  • The thunderstorm is replaced by a dipole with charges $$-q$$ and $$+q$$ separated by $$2h.$$
  • Far above the cloud, the dipole-axis approximation is the clean shortcut.
  • The field scales like $$1/z^3,$$ so doubling altitude cuts the field by a factor of eight.
Quick Anchors
Model
Treat the cloud-ground charge separation as a vertical dipole with charges $$-q$$ and $$+q.$$
Dipole Moment
The separation is $$2h,$$ so the dipole strength is tied to $$q(2h).$$
Axis Field
At altitude $$z$$ far above the cloud, the field uses the axial dipole form shown in the textbook sample.
Altitude Effect
Because $$E\propto z^{-3},$$ a modest increase in altitude causes a sharp field drop.
Rooms

Room 1: Start with the Physical Story

  • Sprites are luminous flashes seen high above major thunderstorms.
  • The sample problem asks whether a storm can produce a strong enough field far above the cloud.
  • So we need a field model for the whole storm, not the microscopic details of each charge pocket.

Room 2: Replace the Storm by a Vertical Dipole

  • The textbook idealization places $$-q$$ at cloud height $$h$$ and $$+q$$ the same distance below ground.
  • That makes the total separation between the charges equal to $$2h.$$
  • This is the dipole picture that captures the large-scale field outside the storm region.

Room 3: Choose the Observation Point

  • The field is requested at altitudes $$z_1=30\,\text{km}$$ and $$z_2=60\,\text{km}.$$
  • Both locations lie on the dipole axis above the storm.
  • That is why the axial dipole formula is the natural shortcut.

Room 4: Write the Far-Axis Dipole Field

  • For this sample, the field high above the dipole is written as $$E=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q(2h)}{z^3}.$$
  • You can also see the same relation as $$E=\dfrac{1}{\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qh}{z^3}.$$
  • The key structural idea is not the constant in front, but the dipole signature: strength over $$z^3.$$

Room 5: Insert the Data Once

  • Use $$q=200\,\text{C}$$ and $$h=3.0\,\text{km},$$ so $$2h=6.0\times10^3\,\text{m}.$$
  • Keep all distances in meters before substitution.
  • That gives one reusable numerator and two different altitude denominators.

Room 6: Evaluate the 30 km Field

  • Set $$z_1=30\times10^3\,\text{m}.$$
  • Substituting into the sample's dipole formula gives $$E_1=1.6\times10^3\,\text{N/C}.$$
  • That is the stronger of the two answers because the point is closer to the storm.

Room 7: Evaluate the 60 km Field

  • Now set $$z_2=60\times10^3\,\text{m}.$$
  • The same formula gives $$E_2=2.0\times10^2\,\text{N/C}.$$
  • The structure is identical; only the cubic distance term changes.

Room 8: Use the Ratio Instead of Recomputing

  • Since $$E\propto 1/z^3,$$ doubling the altitude multiplies the denominator by $$2^3=8.$$
  • So the second field must be one-eighth of the first.
  • That ratio check confirms that $$1.6\times10^3\,\text{N/C}$$ drops to $$2.0\times10^2\,\text{N/C}.$$

Room 9: Main Concept

  • Gauss's-law symmetry pages taught us to compress charge distributions into simple geometric models when symmetry allows it.
  • This sample does the same thing with a thunderstorm by upgrading it into an effective dipole.
  • The lasting memory is that dipole fields fade much faster with distance than point-charge fields.
