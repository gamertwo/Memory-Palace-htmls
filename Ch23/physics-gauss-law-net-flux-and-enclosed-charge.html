Gauss's Law and Net Flux

Gauss's law ties together the two main quantities of this chapter: total electric flux through a closed Gaussian surface and the net charge enclosed by that surface. Once that connection is locked in, many sample problems become a quick question of which charges lie inside the chosen boundary.

$$\Phi=\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A,\qquad \varepsilon_0\Phi=q_{\mathrm{enc}},\qquad \oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A=\frac{q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Core Read

  • Closed-surface flux and enclosed charge are proportional.
  • Only the net enclosed charge matters.
  • Charges outside the surface do not appear in $$q_{\mathrm{enc}}.$$
Quick Anchors
Flux Form
$$\Phi=\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A$$ is the total electric flux through a closed surface.
Gauss Form
$$\varepsilon_0\Phi=q_{\mathrm{enc}}$$ links flux directly to enclosed charge.
Key Filter
Only charges inside the Gaussian surface count in the sum for $$q_{\mathrm{enc}}.$$
Sample Result
For the enclosed charges shown, the net flux is negative because the enclosed charge sum is negative.
Rooms

Room 1: State the Law Plainly

  • Gauss's law says that net electric flux through a closed surface is related to the net charge enclosed by that surface.
  • The Gaussian surface can be real or imaginary, but it must be closed.
  • This law is one of the main structural laws of electrostatics.

Room 2: Store the Equivalent Forms

  • $$\Phi=\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A$$ defines the closed-surface flux.
  • $$\varepsilon_0\Phi=q_{\mathrm{enc}}$$ is the compact proportional form.
  • $$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A=\frac{q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$ is the form most often used in problems.

Room 3: Focus on Net Charge, Not Every Charge

  • All enclosed charges add algebraically to make $$q_{\mathrm{enc}}.$$
  • Positive charges raise the total and negative charges reduce it.
  • External charges do not appear in the enclosed-charge sum.

Room 4: Sample Problem Setup

  • A Gaussian surface wraps around some charged plastic lumps and misses others.
  • The enclosed charges are $$q_1=+3.1\ \mathrm{nC},\ q_2=-5.9\ \mathrm{nC},\ q_3=-3.1\ \mathrm{nC}.$$
  • The charges $$q_4$$ and $$q_5$$ lie outside, so they are ignored.

Room 5: Add Only the Enclosed Charges

  • Compute the enclosed total:
  • $$q_{\mathrm{enc}}=q_1+q_2+q_3=+3.1-5.9-3.1\ \mathrm{nC}=-5.9\ \mathrm{nC}.$$
  • The negative sign already hints that the net flux will be negative.

Room 6: Convert Enclosed Charge into Flux

  • Use Gauss's law directly:
  • $$\Phi=\frac{q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}=\frac{-5.9\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm C}{8.85\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}}.$$
  • That gives a negative result with units of $$\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}.$$

Room 7: Final Numerical Answer

  • $$\Phi\approx -670\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}.$$
  • The negative sign means net inward flux through the chosen surface.
  • That matches the fact that the enclosed charge sum is net negative.

Room 8: Main Lesson of the Example

  • The problem never asked for the field at each point on the surface.
  • It only asked for net flux, so the enclosed-charge sum was enough.
  • This is the power of Gauss's law when the quantity asked is total flux or total enclosed charge.
Law and Example

Gauss's Law Forms

These are the three expressions to keep together in memory. They say the same thing, but each one is useful in a slightly different problem format.

$$\Phi=\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A$$
$$\varepsilon_0\Phi=q_{\mathrm{enc}}$$
$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A=\frac{q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Fast Reading Rule

If the problem gives charge and asks for net flux, divide by $$\varepsilon_0.$$ If the problem gives net flux and asks for enclosed charge, multiply by $$\varepsilon_0.$$

Sample Problem Picture

The surface loops around only three of the five charges. That means the entire flux calculation is controlled by those enclosed charges alone.

What Stays Outside the Math

Charges outside the Gaussian surface may affect the field locally, but they do not appear in the enclosed-charge total used by Gauss's law.

Why the Sign Matters

A negative enclosed charge produces negative net flux, which means that, overall, the field lines cross the surface inward more than outward.
